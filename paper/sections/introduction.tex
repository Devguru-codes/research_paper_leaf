\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Medicinal plants are identified in the field mostly from their leaves, and errors are not equally
costly: confusing two culinary herbs is harmless, but confusing a remedy with a toxic look-alike, such
as \textit{Nerium oleander} with other Apocynaceae, can be dangerous~\citep{farkhondeh2020oleander}.
Deep networks report 97.5--99.7\% accuracy on Indian medicinal leaf
benchmarks~\citep{roopashree2021deepherb,ffncacnn2025}. Those numbers, however, are obtained on
single leaves photographed on white backgrounds, typically with random image-level splits. They say
little about how the models behave when the same species is photographed in the field, against soil,
tiles or cloth, with a phone camera, which is where such a classifier would be used.

Botanists describe a leaf by its geometry: how wide the blade is along its length, where it tapers,
how ragged its margin is, how its veins branch. These characters are measured \emph{at particular
places} on the leaf. Standard CNN and ViT classifiers average their features over the whole image and
have no built-in notion of where the leaf is, let alone of localised geometry. A natural hypothesis is
therefore that architectures which measure leaf geometry locally, and look at the image where they
measure, should transfer better from studio to field. Recent work fuses topological and morphological
summaries with deep features~\citep{phgnet2023,tdavit2026,gu2026multifiltration,blaser2026encoding},
but usually as global descriptors, and without controls that would tell whether the geometry itself
is what helps.

We test this hypothesis with a ladder of matched models that share backbone, data, vein head and
training, and differ in one ingredient at a time: (1)~a plain classifier; (2)~an extra branch on
mid-level patch features; (3)~the same branch pooled over the leaf mask, i.e.\ knowing \emph{where}
the leaf is; (4)~localised pooling over strips of the image or over slabs of an object-centred
directional sweep; and (5)~\emph{sweep-valuation attention} (\SVA{}), which we introduce as the most
structured member of the family: for each slab of the sweep it measures how much area and boundary
length a learned, appearance-weighted cubical complex of the blade and venation adds there, and lets
that measurement attend to the image patches of the same slab. A global fusion of the same measurements
tests whether geometry helps without localisation.

The answer is clear and partly negative. On ConvNeXt-T the studio-to-field gain over a plain model is
large (12--14 points) and splits into three parts: mid-level features (small), knowing where the leaf
is (which matters only when the background is present), and pooling locally within the leaf. Measured
geometry, whether fused globally or attached to localised tokens, adds little if anything. The gain is
small on EfficientNet-B0, absent on ViTs, and irrelevant for a biology foundation model, which
transfers far better on its own.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A controlled decomposition of studio-to-field transfer} for medicinal leaves on five
  backbones, with up to 5 folds $\times$ 5 seeds, planned and post-hoc contrast families with Holm
  correction, equivalence tests and a bootstrap over runs and field species. Leaf location ($+4.0$) and
  localised pooling ($+4.6$ to $+6.2$) are the two significant ingredients on raw field photographs of
  ImageNet ConvNeXt-T, and both add to strong background-compositing augmentation. A
  pre-registered replication on LeafSnap30 tests the account on a second studio-to-field pair.
\item \textbf{Sweep-valuation attention}, a differentiable, backbone-agnostic module that turns a
  directional filtration of appearance-weighted cubical complexes into slab tokens, and a careful
  negative result: its measured geometry does not detectably improve transfer beyond localised pooling,
  its slab-restricted attention can be replaced by global attention, and Euler-characteristic tokens
  make models sensitive to topology but transfer worse.
\item \textbf{A leakage-audited protocol and probes} for the Medicinal Leaf Dataset: duplicate and
  re-shot grouping, a corruption suite (MedLeaf-C), a leaf cue-conflict set, a perceptually matched
  topology-destruction test and a checked mapping of 20 DIMPSAR field species.
\end{enumerate}
Code, splits, probes, statistics and the outputs of all training runs are released.
